Part 1: What is a linear program?

Where We've Been

Where This Goes

Each family came with its own tricks. Linear programming is a single framework that can express flow, matching and cover problems — exactly for some, as a relaxation (a bound) for others — and a single solver handles every LP.

This week Learn to write problems as LPs and to see what an LP looks like geometrically. Next time: duality, the theory that explains our Week 8 certificates.

The three ingredients

A linear program (LP) is built from:

  • Decision variables $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \R$ — the numbers we get to choose
  • A linear objective to maximise or minimise: $c_1 x_1 + \cdots + c_n x_n$
  • Linear constraints: each is $a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n \;\{\le, =, \ge\}\; b$

The three ingredients

Example with concrete numbers (we will reuse it all session):

$$\begin{aligned} \max\quad & 3x + 2y \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x \le 6, \qquad x + y \le 8, \qquad x + 3y \le 18 \\ & x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Story: a workshop makes $x$ chairs (profit 3) and $y$ tables (profit 2). Only chairs need the machine, and it can finish at most 6 of them; each piece uses one unit of wood (8 available); a table takes three times as much varnish as a chair (18 available).

What "linear" allows — and forbids

Allowed

  • $2x_1 - 5x_2 + x_3 \le 7$
  • $\sum_{e : v \in e} x_e \le 1$
  • fractional values: $x = \tfrac{1}{2}$ is fine

Not allowed

  • $x_1 x_2 \le 3$ (products of variables)
  • $x^2$, $|x|$, $\max(x,y)$ in constraints
  • $x \in \{0,1\}$ (integrality!)

What "linear" allows — and forbids

Careful Requiring $x \in \{0,1\}$ or $x \in \mathbb{Z}$ makes it an integer linear program (ILP) — a different beast. ILP is NP-hard (Week 11 will make that precise); LP is solvable in polynomial time. The gap between them is the story of Parts 4 and 5.

Standard form

Collect the coefficients into a matrix and vectors: $A \in \R^{m \times n}$, $b \in \R^m$, $c \in \R^n$.

Definition An LP in standard form is $$\max\ \{\, c^\top x \;:\; Ax \le b,\ x \ge 0 \,\}$$ $n$ variables, $m$ inequality constraints, all variables nonnegative.

Standard form

Our example, in matrix clothing:

$$c = \begin{pmatrix}3\\2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad b = \begin{pmatrix}6\\8\\18\end{pmatrix}$$

Row $i$ of $A$ paired with $b_i$ is one constraint: row 2 says $1\cdot x + 1 \cdot y \le 8$.

Every LP can be put in standard form

Standard form looks restrictive. It isn't — four mechanical rewrites:

You haveRewrite as
$\min\ c^\top x$$\max\ (-c)^\top x$   (negate the answer back)
$a^\top x \ge \beta$$(-a)^\top x \le -\beta$

Every LP can be put in standard form

You haveRewrite as
$a^\top x = \beta$$a^\top x \le \beta$ and $(-a)^\top x \le -\beta$
$x_j$ unrestricted$x_j = x_j^+ - x_j^-$ with $x_j^+, x_j^- \ge 0$

So standard form loses nothing: theorems proved and solvers written for this one shape apply to every LP.

Conversion: a worked example

Given ($x_2$ unrestricted):

$$\begin{aligned} \min\quad & 2x_1 - x_2\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_1 + x_2 = 4\\ & x_1 - 3x_2 \ge -6\\ & x_1 \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Standard form (sub $x_2 = u - v$):

$$\begin{aligned} \max\quad & -2x_1 + u - v\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_1 + u - v \le 4\\ & -x_1 - u + v \le -4\\ & -x_1 + 3u - 3v \le 6\\ & x_1, u, v \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Sanity check: $(x_1, x_2) = (3, 1)$, i.e. $(x_1, u, v) = (3, 1, 0)$, is feasible in both, objective $5$ on the left $=$ $-5$ on the right (sign flipped by the $\min \to \max$ swap). ✓

Part 2: Three old friends as LPs

max flow · bipartite matching · vertex cover

Maximum flow

One variable per edge: $f_e$ = flow on $e$.

Five edges, so five numbers to choose: $f_{sa}, f_{sb}, f_{ab}, f_{at}, f_{bt}$.

Maximum flow

A flow must obey two kinds of rules — and both are linear:

  • Capacity: $0 \le f_e \le c_e$
  • Conservation: flow in $=$ flow out at every $v \ne s, t$

Objective: push as much out of $s$ as possible.

Max flow: the LP

In general ($\delta^+(v)$ = edges out of $v$, $\delta^-(v)$ = edges into $v$):

$$\begin{aligned} \max\quad & \textstyle\sum_{e \in \delta^+(s)} f_e\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & \textstyle\sum_{e \in \delta^-(v)} f_e = \sum_{e \in \delta^+(v)} f_e \quad \forall v \ne s,t\\ & 0 \le f_e \le c_e \quad \forall e \in E \end{aligned}$$

Max flow: the LP

For our graph (5 variables):

$$\begin{aligned} \max\quad & f_{sa} + f_{sb}\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & f_{sa} = f_{ab} + f_{at}\\ & f_{sb} + f_{ab} = f_{bt}\\ & 0 \le f_{sa} \le 4,\ 0 \le f_{sb} \le 3\\ & 0 \le f_{ab} \le 2,\ 0 \le f_{at} \le 2,\ 0 \le f_{bt} \le 4 \end{aligned}$$

Max flow: the LP

Optimal value $6$: e.g. $f_{sa}=4$, $f_{at}=2$, $f_{ab}=2$, $f_{sb}=2$, $f_{bt}=4$.

Matches the min cut $\{s,a,b\}$ vs $\{t\}$ of capacity $2+4=6$ — Week 8's certificate, now a statement about two LPs (wait for Week 10!).

Bipartite matching

One variable per edge: $x_e \in [0,1]$, "how much" $e$ is in the matching.

$$\begin{aligned} \max\quad & \textstyle\sum_{e \in E} x_e\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & \textstyle\sum_{e : v \in e} x_e \le 1 \quad \forall v \in V\\ & 0 \le x_e \le 1 \quad \forall e \in E \end{aligned}$$

Bipartite matching

At $v_2$: $x_{u_1 v_2} + x_{u_2 v_2} + x_{u_3 v_2} \le 1$.

A matching is exactly a feasible $x$ with $x_e \in \{0,1\}$ — the LP is the relaxation that lets edges be picked fractionally.

LP vs. maximum matching

Every matching is a feasible LP solution: set $x_e = 1$ on its edges and $0$ elsewhere. So

$$\text{LP value} \;\ge\; \text{maximum matching size}.$$

Can the LP be strictly larger — can fractions beat every genuine matching?

Matching on the triangle

  • All-halves is feasible: each vertex touches two edges, load $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1 \le 1$. ✓ Value $\tfrac32$.
  • But any two edges of a triangle share a vertex, so every matching has $1$ edge. The fractions win: $\tfrac32 > 1$.

Bipartite graphs

Fact For bipartite graphs, nothing is lost: the matching LP always has an integral optimal solution — its optimum equals the maximum matching size. We will see why later today.

Vertex cover

One variable per vertex: $x_v \in [0,1]$, "how much" $v$ is in the cover.

$$\begin{aligned} \min\quad & \textstyle\sum_{v} w_v x_v\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x_u + x_v \ge 1 \quad \forall \{u,v\} \in E\\ & 0 \le x_v \le 1 \quad \forall v \in V \end{aligned}$$

Vertex cover

Here: $\min 3x_a + 2x_b + 2x_c + x_d$ with $x_a + x_b \ge 1$, $x_a + x_c \ge 1$, $x_b + x_d \ge 1$, $x_c + x_d \ge 1$.

Both $\{a,d\}$ and $\{b,c\}$ cost $4$ — and the LP optimum is also $4$ here.

LP vs. minimum vertex cover

Every vertex cover is a feasible LP solution: set $x_v = 1$ on its vertices and $0$ elsewhere. So

$$\text{LP value} \;\le\; \text{minimum cover weight}.$$

Can the LP be strictly smaller — can fractions undercut every genuine cover?

Vertex cover on the triangle

  • All weights $1$. All-halves is feasible: every edge gets $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$. So $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} \le \tfrac32$ — in fact $= \tfrac32$: summing the three edge constraints gives $2(x_A + x_B + x_C) \ge 3$.
  • Any integral cover needs $2$ of the $3$ vertices (one vertex misses the opposite edge). So $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} = \tfrac32 < 2 = \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{ILP}}$.

The modelling pattern

ProblemVariablesConstraints say
max flow$f_e$ per edgecapacities, conservation
matching$x_e$ per edgeeach vertex used $\le 1$ time
vertex cover$x_v$ per vertexeach edge covered $\ge 1$ time
  • Choose variables = "the decisions", one number each.
  • Every combinatorial rule became a linear inequality or equation.

The modelling pattern

  • Matching and vertex cover needed a relaxation: $\{0,1\} \rightsquigarrow [0,1]$. Max flow didn't — flows were already fractional-friendly.

Part 3: The geometry of an LP

two variables, so we can draw everything

Our running example

$$\begin{aligned} \max\quad & 3x + 2y\\ \text{s.t.}\quad & x \le 6 && \text{(machine)}\\ & x + y \le 8 && \text{(wood)}\\ & x + 3y \le 18 && \text{(varnish)}\\ & x, y \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Two variables $\Rightarrow$ every solution is a point $(x, y)$ in the plane.

Our running example

  • Each constraint $a_1 x + a_2 y \le \beta$ keeps a half-plane: the line $a_1 x + a_2 y = \beta$ plus everything on one side of it.
  • Feasible set $=$ intersection of all five half-planes.

Building the feasible region

$x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$: stay in the first quadrant.

■ varnish: below $x+3y=18$

■ wood: below $x+y=8$

■ machine: left of $x=6$

The intersection is a convex polygon — every feasible production plan lives inside it.

Polytopes and vertices

Definition A polyhedron is an intersection of finitely many half-spaces, $\{x \in \R^n : Ax \le b\}$. A bounded polyhedron is a polytope.
Definition A point $z$ of a polyhedron $P$ is a vertex (extreme point) if it is not the midpoint of any segment inside $P$: there is no $d \ne 0$ with both $z + d \in P$ and $z - d \in P$.

No two-way wiggle room

Can $z$ step to both $z + d$ and $z - d$?

Inside: every direction works both ways. Not a vertex.

On an edge: slide along it both ways. One such $d$ is enough — not a vertex.

Corner: every $d$ has one side (red) leaving the region. A vertex.

Polytopes and vertices

  • Equivalent, more computational view in $\R^2$: a vertex is where $2$ constraint lines meet (in $\R^n$: $n$ constraints hold with equality, with linearly independent rows) — and the point is feasible.
  • Our region is a polytope with $5$ vertices. Let's find them all.

The five vertices

Each vertex $=$ two tight constraints:

  • $(0,0)$: $x = 0$, $y = 0$
  • $(6,0)$: $x = 6$, $y = 0$
  • $(6,2)$: $x = 6$, $x + y = 8$
  • $(3,5)$: $x + y = 8$, $x + 3y = 18$
  • $(0,6)$: $x = 0$, $x + 3y = 18$

The five vertices

How do we compute a vertex? Solve the $2 \times 2$ linear system of its two tight constraints.

e.g. solving $x + y = 8$ and $x + 3y = 18$: subtract to get $2y = 10$, so $y = 5$ — the vertex $(3,5)$.

Sweeping the objective

Every level set $3x + 2y = k$ is a line perpendicular to $c = (3,2)$. Increasing $k$ slides the line in direction $c$.

$k = 6$: cuts through the region

$k = 12$: still inside…

$k = 18$: getting tight…

$k = 22$: touches the region at exactly one point — the vertex $(6,2)$. Push further and we leave the region entirely.

Reading off the answer

Cross-check by evaluating $3x + 2y$ at all five vertices:

vertextight constraints$3x + 2y$
$(0,0)$$x=0,\ y=0$$0$
$(6,0)$$x=6,\ y=0$$18$
$(6,2)$$x=6,\ x+y=8$$\mathbf{22}$
$(3,5)$$x+y=8,\ x+3y=18$$19$
$(0,6)$$x=0,\ x+3y=18$$12$

Reading off the answer

The last level line touching the region pins down the optimum — and the vertex table agrees.

Optimum $22$ at $(6,2)$: make $6$ chairs, $2$ tables; the machine is at capacity and the wood is used up, and $6 + 3\cdot 2 = 12 < 18$ varnish left slack.

Why is the optimum at a vertex?

Keep walking until you can't. Start at any feasible $z$.

  • If $z$ is not a vertex, some $d \ne 0$ has $z \pm d$ feasible. Pick the sign with $c^\top d \ge 0$ and walk until a new constraint becomes tight. The objective never drops.
  • Repeat. In 2D: slide across the region to an edge, then along the edge to a corner.

Why is the optimum at a vertex?

Why the walk ends at a vertex within $n$ steps:

  • Constraints tight at $z$ stay tight: each has $a^\top d = 0$, or $z + d$ or $z - d$ would break it.
  • The new tight constraint has $a^\top d \ne 0$, so it is independent of the old ones.
  • Each step adds an independent tight constraint — after at most $n$ steps, we are at a vertex.

Can the walk get stuck?

But does the walk always reach a new constraint? Two cases.

Case 1: $c^\top d \ne 0$ Pick the sign with $c^\top d > 0$ and walk. Either a constraint stops us — progress — or we walk forever with $c^\top x \to \infty$: the LP is unbounded.

Can the walk get stuck?

Case 2: $c^\top d = 0$ The objective is flat, so either sign will do. But what if neither direction ever hits a constraint?
  • Then the whole line $\{\, z + t\,d : t \in \R \,\}$ lies inside the region.
  • But some $d_j \ne 0$, so $z_j + t\,d_j < 0$ once $t$ goes far enough one way — breaking $x_j \ge 0$.
  • So one of $\pm d$ does hit a constraint.

Can the walk get stuck?

So the walk only fails if the region is empty (nowhere to start) or the LP is unbounded (Case 1 runs forever). That's the fine print in the theorem.

Careful Case 2 needed $x \ge 0$. Without it, the strip $\{(x,y) : 0 \le y \le 1\}$ with $\max y$ has optimum $1$ — but no vertices at all.

The fundamental theorem

Fundamental theorem of LP For an LP $\max\{c^\top x : Ax \le b,\ x \ge 0\}$, exactly one of the following holds:
  1. the LP is infeasible (the region is empty), or
  2. the LP is unbounded (feasible points of arbitrarily large value exist), or
  3. the LP has a finite optimum, and some vertex of the feasible region is optimal.

The fundamental theorem

Consequence: an LP — a continuous problem over infinitely many points — is secretly a finite problem: only the vertices matter.

(Ties are allowed: if $c$ is perpendicular to an edge, the whole edge is optimal — e.g. objective $x + y$ in our example is $8$ along the entire segment from $(6,2)$ to $(3,5)$. A vertex is still among the optima.)

The other two outcomes, drawn

Careful "Unbounded region" $\ne$ "unbounded LP": if $c$ points away from the open end, the optimum is still finite and still at a vertex. It's the objective that has to escape.

An algorithm for tiny LPs: vertex enumeration

The fundamental theorem is an algorithm — check every vertex. With $n = 2$ variables and $m$ constraints (counting $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$):

best ← −∞,  argbest ← none
for each pair {i, j} of constraints:            // O(m²) candidate corners
    solve   aᵢ·(x,y) = bᵢ  and  aⱼ·(x,y) = bⱼ     // a 2×2 linear system
    if the lines are parallel: continue
    if (x,y) satisfies ALL m constraints:          // feasibility check, O(m)
        if c·(x,y) > best:  best ← c·(x,y),  argbest ← (x,y)
return argbest, best                             // none found ⇔ infeasible

An algorithm for tiny LPs: vertex enumeration

  • Caveat: on an unbounded LP this still returns the best vertex that exists — a confidently finite, wrong answer. Enumeration assumes a bounded optimum.
  • Running time $O(m^3)$ — fine for $m \le 100$, hopeless in general: in $\R^n$ there can be $\binom{m}{n}$ candidates, exponentially many.

What's used in practice

Fact Linear programming is solvable in polynomial time (in the bit-size of the input). So from now on: if a problem can be written exactly as an LP of polynomial size — polynomially many variables and constraints — then we can solve it efficiently.

Part 4: Relaxations and the integrality gap

The LP as a bound

Part 2 showed both directions:

  • Minimisation (vertex cover): $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} \le \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{ILP}}$ — the LP is an efficiently computable lower bound.
  • Maximisation (matching): $\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} \ge \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{ILP}}$ — an upper bound.

Question: how far apart can they be? If never far, the LP is a great proxy — the engine behind Week 12's approximation algorithms.

Integrality gap

Definition The integrality gap of an LP relaxation (of a minimisation problem) is the smallest $\alpha$ such that $$\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{ILP}} \;\le\; \alpha \cdot \mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} \qquad \text{on every instance}$$ — the worst-case factor by which the true optimum exceeds the LP bound.

Gap $= 1$ means the relaxation is exact: the LP always has an integral optimum.

Vertex cover gap

  • The triangle shows the gap is $\ge \tfrac{2}{3/2} = \tfrac43.$
  • The complete graph $K_n$ pushes it to $\tfrac{n-1}{n/2} \to 2.$

Can it be worse than $2$? No — and the proof is short.

Vertex cover gap ≤ 2

Take an optimal LP solution $x^*$ and round: $\;C = \{\, v : x^*_v \ge \tfrac12 \,\}$.

  • $C$ is a cover. Every edge has $x^*_u + x^*_v \ge 1$, so one endpoint has $x^* \ge \tfrac12$ and lands in $C$.
  • $C$ costs at most twice the LP. $$\begin{aligned} w(C) = \textstyle\sum_{v \in C} w_v &\le \textstyle\sum_{v \in C} w_v \cdot 2x^*_v\\ &\le 2\textstyle\sum_{v} w_v x^*_v = 2\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}} \end{aligned}$$ (each $v \in C$ has $2x^*_v \ge 1$; then add the $v \notin C$ terms, all $\ge 0$)

Vertex cover gap ≤ 2

$C$ is an integral cover, so on every instance $$\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{ILP}} \;\le\; w(C) \;\le\; 2\,\mathrm{OPT}_{\mathrm{LP}}.$$ The gap is at most $2$ — and $K_n$ shows it's exactly $2$.

Bonus: solve the LP, round — a polynomial-time cover within $2\times$ optimal. Week 12 returns to this as an approximation algorithm.

Part 5: Integral polytopes and total unimodularity

Geometry of the gap

The dictionary between Parts 3 and 4:

  • The LP optimum lives at a vertex of the relaxation's polytope.
  • If every vertex is a $0/1$ point (an integral polytope), solving the LP is solving the combinatorial problem.

Geometry of the gap

  • The triangle's cover polytope has the genuinely fractional vertex $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ — a corner that no integer point can imitate. Gaps are geometric defects: fractional corners.

So a natural question: which polytopes from combinatorics have all corners integral? Two answers today: one from linear algebra (next), and one from Week 5 (Part 6).

Bipartite graphs: gap 1

Fact On bipartite graphs, both the matching LP and the vertex cover LP have integrality gap $1$: every vertex of their polytopes is integral. Both triangle examples hinge on an odd cycle — bipartite graphs have none. Next time, combined with duality, this gives König's theorem.

Totally unimodular

Definition A matrix $A$ is totally unimodular (TU) if every square submatrix has determinant $0$, $+1$ or $-1$.
Theorem If $A$ is TU and $b$ is integral, every vertex of $\{\, x : Ax \le b,\ x \ge 0 \,\}$ is integral.

Cramer's rule

For a square system $Bx = {\color{#b85a26} b'}$ with $\det B \ne 0$:

$$x_j = \frac{\det B_j}{\det B}$$

where $B_j$ is $B$ with column $j$ replaced by ${\color{#b85a26} b'}$.

Example: the vertex $(3,5)$ of our pentagon, where $x + y = 8$ and $x + 3y = 18$ are tight:

$$B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad {\color{#b85a26} b'} = \begin{pmatrix} {\color{#b85a26} 8} \\ {\color{#b85a26} 18} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \det B = 2.$$

Cramer's rule

Replace one column of $B$ by ${\color{#b85a26} b'}$ at a time:

$$x = \frac{\det \begin{pmatrix} {\color{#b85a26} 8} & 1\\ {\color{#b85a26} 18} & 3 \end{pmatrix}}{2} = \frac{24 - 18}{2} = 3$$
$$y = \frac{\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & {\color{#b85a26} 8}\\ 1 & {\color{#b85a26} 18} \end{pmatrix}}{2} = \frac{18 - 8}{2} = 5$$

The only division is by $\det B$. (Here $\det B = 2$, and the numerators happened to be even.)

Why TU gives integers

  • Part 3: a vertex is where $n$ linearly independent constraints are tight. So it solves $Bx = b'$ for a nonsingular square $B$ built from rows of the constraints, with $b'$ integral.
  • Cramer's rule: $\;x_j = \dfrac{\det B_j}{\det B}$. The numerator is an integer.
  • The denominator is $\pm 1$: rows from $x \ge 0$ are unit vectors, and expanding along them leaves a square submatrix of $A$.

So every coordinate of every vertex is an integer.

The incidence matrix

The vertex–edge incidence matrix $A$ of a graph has one row per vertex and one column per edge: $A_{v,e} = 1$ if $v$ is an endpoint of $e$, else $0$.

$$\begin{array}{r|ccc} & ab & bc & ca\\ \hline a & 1 & 0 & 1\\ b & 1 & 1 & 0\\ c & 0 & 1 & 1 \end{array}$$

Every column has exactly two $1$s: the edge's two endpoints.

The incidence matrix

Both of our LPs are built from it:

  • Matching: row $v$ says $\sum_{e : v \in e} x_e \le 1$. All together: $Ax \le \mathbf{1}$.
  • Vertex cover: column $e = uv$ says $x_u + x_v \ge 1$. All together: $A^\top x \ge \mathbf{1}$.

So whether their polytopes have integral corners depends on the determinants of $A$'s square submatrices.

The triangle is not TU

The triangle's incidence matrix has determinant $2$:

$$\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1\\ 1 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1 \cdot (1 - 0) - 0 + 1 \cdot (1 - 0) = 2.$$

The vertex cover LP's corner has all three edge constraints tight: $x_a + x_b = x_b + x_c = x_a + x_c = 1$. Cramer divides by $2$ — that's where the $\tfrac12$'s come from.

Bipartite graphs are TU

Claim: the incidence matrix of a bipartite graph (sides $L$, $R$) is TU. Induct on the size of a square submatrix $B$. Each column has at most two $1$s — one in an $L$ row, one in an $R$ row.

  • A column of zeros: $\det B = 0$.
  • A column with one $1$: expand along it; $\det B = \pm$ a smaller determinant, which is $0$ or $\pm1$ by induction.
  • Every column has two $1$s: the $L$ rows and the $R$ rows each sum to all-ones, so ($L$ rows) $-$ ($R$ rows) $= 0$. The rows are dependent: $\det B = 0$.

Bipartite graphs are TU

The last case on the 4-cycle: $L = \{{\color{#2e588f} a_1, a_2}\}$, $R = \{{\color{#4d7349} b_1, b_2}\}$.

$$\begin{array}{r|cccc} & a_1b_1 & b_1a_2 & a_2b_2 & b_2a_1\\ \hline {\color{#2e588f} a_1} & {\color{#2e588f} 1} & {\color{#2e588f} 0} & {\color{#2e588f} 0} & {\color{#2e588f} 1}\\ {\color{#2e588f} a_2} & {\color{#2e588f} 0} & {\color{#2e588f} 1} & {\color{#2e588f} 1} & {\color{#2e588f} 0}\\ {\color{#4d7349} b_1} & {\color{#4d7349} 1} & {\color{#4d7349} 1} & {\color{#4d7349} 0} & {\color{#4d7349} 0}\\ {\color{#4d7349} b_2} & {\color{#4d7349} 0} & {\color{#4d7349} 0} & {\color{#4d7349} 1} & {\color{#4d7349} 1} \end{array}$$

${\color{#2e588f} a_1 + a_2} = (1,1,1,1) = {\color{#4d7349} b_1 + b_2}$, so the rows are dependent: $\det = 0$.

Bipartite graphs are TU

  • The matching LP uses $A$; the vertex cover LP uses $A^\top$ with $\ge$ rows. Transposing and negating keep TU.
  • So on bipartite graphs both polytopes have integral corners: gap $1$, as the Fact promised.
  • The odd cycle breaks exactly the last case: the triangle's rows can't be split into $L$ and $R$.

Part 6: Kruskal and the forest polytope

Forests as vectors

  • Week 5: Kruskal adds edges smallest weight first, skipping any edge that closes a cycle, and returns an MST. Correct because forests form a matroid.
  • Encode an edge set $F \subseteq E$ as its indicator vector $\mathbb{1}_F \in \{0,1\}^E$ — one coordinate per edge, like our matching LPs.

The goal

We want linear constraints such that

  1. Valid: every forest satisfies them, and
  2. No extra corners: every vertex of the polytope is a forest.

Together: the polytope is exactly the convex hull of the forests. Then MST is an LP — and we can watch what Kruskal does to it.

The goal

  • Checking (1) is easy: count edges.
  • To see (2) fail: find an objective where some feasible point beats every forest. The LP optimum sits at a vertex — and that vertex can't be a forest.

Build it on the smallest graph with a cycle: the triangle. Three edges, so $x \in \R^3$ — we can draw it.

Edge sets of a triangle

  • $2^3 = 8$ edge sets $=$ the $8$ corners of the cube.
  • $7$ are forests. Only $\{ab, bc, ca\}$ — the whole triangle — is a cycle.

First try: the cube

  • Every forest satisfies $0 \le x_e \le 1$, so (1) ✓. Try the cube.
  • (2) ✗: maximise $x_{ab} + x_{bc} + x_{ca}$. Every forest scores at most $2$, but the LP answers $(1,1,1)$ — the cycle, scoring $3$.

We need a constraint that keeps (1) and cuts off the cycle.

Cut off the cycle

A forest uses at most $2$ of the triangle's $3$ edges, so this is valid — (1) ✓:

$$x_{ab} + x_{bc} + x_{ca} \le 2$$ The plane slices off the red corner.

It creates no new corners: it meets the cube only at $3$ existing ones.

Corners left: exactly the $7$ forests — (2) ✓.

One cut per cycle?

Natural guess: for every cycle $C$, $\ \sum_{e \in C} x_e \le |C| - 1$. Valid — (1) ✓.

  • Each triangle: $3 \cdot \tfrac23 = 2 \le 2$. ✓
  • Each $4$-cycle: $4 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac83 \le 3$. ✓
  • Maximise $\sum_e x_e$: this point scores $6 \cdot \tfrac23 = 4$, but a forest on $4$ vertices has at most $3$ edges. (2) ✗

What the cycles miss

The $4$-cycle constraint counts only the cycle's own edges: $4 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac83 \le 3$.

It ignores the chords. But a forest can't use more than $3$ edges of any kind among these $4$ vertices — and all $6$ edges give $4 > 3$.

Repair: count every edge inside the cycle's vertex set. (The triangle has no chords — which is why it couldn't tell the difference.)

Count by vertices

  • Week 5: a forest with $k$ edges on $s$ vertices has $s - k$ components.
  • At least one component, so $k \le s - 1$.
  • The edges of a forest that lie inside a vertex set $S$ form a forest on $S$ — so these are valid, (1) ✓: $$\textstyle\sum_{e \subseteq S} x_e \;\le\; |S| - 1 \qquad \forall S \subseteq V,\ |S| \ge 2$$

($e \subseteq S$: both endpoints of $e$ lie in $S$.)

Count by vertices

Sanity check — it includes every earlier fix:

  • $S = \{a, b, c\}$ in the triangle: $x_{ab} + x_{bc} + x_{ca} \le 2$.
  • $S = $ all of $K_4$: $\sum_e x_e \le 3$ — kills the $\tfrac23$ point, whose total is $4$.
  • $S = \{u, v\}$: the only edge inside is $uv$, so $x_{uv} \le 1$ — the cube's upper bounds are special cases.

Does it get (2) in general? That's the theorem.

The forest polytope

Theorem (Edmonds) These constraints — the subtour elimination constraints — are enough:
$$\begin{aligned}P_{\text{forest}} = \Big\{ x \in \R^E \;:\;\; & x \ge 0,\\[-0.1em] & \textstyle\sum_{e \subseteq S} x_e \le |S| - 1 \ \ \forall S \subseteq V,\ |S| \ge 2 \Big\}\end{aligned}$$
is exactly the convex hull of the forests of $G$. Every vertex is a forest — the polytope is integral. (We won't prove this here.)

The forest polytope

  • Integral, but not by TU: rows $\{1,2,3\}, \{1,2,4\}, \{1,3,4\}$ of $K_4$ on columns $12, 13, 14$ give determinant $-2$. Here integrality comes from the matroid structure.
  • Exponentially many constraints — and that's allowed! A polytope doesn't care how many inequalities describe it. (Solving such LPs efficiently needs the ellipsoid method + a "separation oracle" — a story for another course.)
  • For spanning trees of a connected $G$: add $\sum_{e \in E} x_e = |V| - 1$; the vertices become exactly the spanning trees.

MST as an LP

Pick $C$ bigger than every weight and set $w'_e = C - w_e > 0$.

  • Every spanning tree has $|V| - 1$ edges, so $w'(T) = (|V|-1)\,C - w(T)$: maximising $w'$ is minimising $w$.
  • All $w'_e > 0$, so a best forest never stops short of spanning.

So the MST is the optimal vertex of $\max\, \{ w'^\top x : x \in P_{\text{forest}} \}$. Largest $w'$ first is smallest $w$ first: Kruskal's order.

Kruskal walks the polytope

  • Start at $\varnothing$: value $0.$
  • Take $ab$: to $\{ab\}$, value $3.$
  • Take $bc$: to $\{ab, bc\}$, value $5.$
  • $ca$ closes a cycle — that corner is cut off. Stop.

Why it stops at the optimum

Neighbours of $\{ab, bc\}$ on the polytope:

  • Drop an edge: $\{ab\}$ (value $3$), $\{bc\}$ (value $2$).
  • Swap one edge for another along the cut face: $\{ab, ca\}$ (value $4$), $\{bc, ca\}$ (value $3$).

None beats $5$. On any LP, a vertex with no better neighbouring vertex is optimal — convexity leaves no local traps (we won't prove this here).

Why it stops at the optimum

Takeaway Kruskal is a simplex-style walk on $P_{\text{forest}}$: each accepted edge is a step to an adjacent, better vertex.
  • Edmonds' theorem makes the polytope integral, so its optimal vertex is a real spanning tree.
  • The exchange property is why greedy gets there by only adding edges — it never has to back up.
