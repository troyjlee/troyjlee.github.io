Previous best: about $1.3^n$ (PPSZ and refinements).

3‑SAT as a polynomial

The clause $x_a \lor \lnot x_b \lor x_c$ fails only at $x_a = 0,\ x_b = 1,\ x_c = 0$. So $$\Phi(X_a, X_b, X_c) \;=\; 1 - (1 - X_a)\,X_b\,(1 - X_c)$$ is $1$ on satisfying inputs and $0$ otherwise.

In general: $\;\Phi_e = 1 - \prod_{L \in e} (1 - L)$, where the literal $x_v$ becomes $X_v$ and $\lnot x_v$ becomes $1 - X_v$.

Counting with a polynomial

$$\#F \;=\; \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^n} \; \prod_{e \in F} \Phi_e(x)$$
  • Each term is $1$ if $x$ satisfies every clause, and $0$ otherwise.
  • Assume each variable appears in at most 3 clauses (the general case reduces to this). Then the product has degree $\le 3n$.
  • Evaluating the sum one $x$ at a time is brute force: $2^n$ evaluations.

When the sum is easy

Suppose the variables split into groups $S_1, \dots, S_r$ of size $n/r$, and every clause lies inside one group.

  • Then $\#F = N_1 N_2 \cdots N_r$, where $N_g$ counts the satisfying assignments of group $g$'s clauses.
  • Cost: $r \cdot 2^{n/r}$ instead of $2^n$.

Real formulas don't split. Plan: build a polynomial $H(T)$ with $H(0) = \#F$ that is cheap to evaluate at $T = 1, \dots, Q$. Then recover $H(0)$ from those values.

Linear replacement

In clause $e$, replace each input $X_v$ by a linear form in all the variables, with coefficients that are polynomials in $T$: $$X_v \;\leadsto\; \ell_{e,v}(T) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_{e,v,i}(T)\, X_i .$$

  • At $T = 0$ nothing changes: $\ell_{e,v}(0) = X_v$, i.e. $p_{e,v,v}(0) = 1$ and $p_{e,v,i}(0) = 0$ for $i \ne v.$
  • Each coefficient $p_{e,v,i}$ has degree $\le R$ in $T$.
  • Each occurrence gets its own form: $x_v$ may be replaced differently in different clauses.

A polynomial in $T$

$$H(T) \;=\; \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^n} \; \prod_{e \in F} \Phi_e\bigl(\ell_{e,v}(T)\bigr)_{v \in e}$$
  • $H(0) = \#F$: at $T = 0$ every form is the original variable.
  • $H$ is a polynomial in one variable $T$. Its degree is at most $3n \cdot R$, since the inputs enter with degree $\le 3n$ and each has degree $\le R$ in $T$.
  • At $t \ne 0$ the inputs need not be $0$ or $1$, so $H(t)$ counts nothing. It is just a number, and we only need it to be cheap.

Cheap points $T = 1, \dots, Q$

The forms are built so that at each $t \in \{1, \dots, Q\}$:

  • the variables split into $r$ groups; each clause is assigned to one;
  • a clause reads its own group freely, but other groups only through a few shared measurements (small integer linear combinations);
  • fixing the measurement values splits the sum into independent group sums, as on the “easy” slide.

Cost per point: (number of measurement values) $\times\ 2^{O(n/r)} = 2^{o(n)}.$

The degree budget

  • Suppose a term of $\ell_{e,v}$ reaches outside clause $e$'s group only at $t = 2$. Multiply it by $(1 - T/2)$: unchanged at $T = 0$, gone at $T = 2$. Cost: one degree.
  • The lazy fix, $\ell_{e,v}(T) = \prod_{t=1}^{Q} (1 - T/t)\, X_v$, vanishes at every cheap point — but has degree $Q$, and we'll see that costs as much as brute force.
  • The heart of the paper: forms of degree $R = \Theta(\sqrt{\log n})$ with $Q = \Theta(\log n / \log\log n)$ cheap points. So $R \ll Q$.

Interpolation

Fact A polynomial of degree $\le d$ is determined by its values at any $d + 1$ points.

$d = 2$: many parabolas fit $H(1)$ and $H(2)$, each with its own $H(0)$.

Adding $H(3)$ leaves only $T^2 - 4T + 5$, so $H(0) = 5$.

Why: the difference of two such polynomials has degree $\le d$ but $d + 1$ roots. A nonzero polynomial of degree $\le d$ has at most $d$ roots, so the difference is $0$.

Interpolation in $T$

We want $H(0)$, but $H$ is cheap only at $T = 1, \dots, Q$.

  • If $Q > d = 3nR$: compute $H(1), \dots, H(Q)$, solve $Q$ linear equations for the coefficients of $H$, and read off $H(0) = \#F$.
  • But $Q = \Theta(\log n / \log\log n) \ll 3nR.$

One point, many coefficients

The Taylor coefficients $c_k = H^{(k)}(t)/k!$ of $H$ at a point $t$: $$H(t + z) \;=\; c_0 + c_1 z + c_2 z^2 + \cdots + c_d z^d.$$

  • They determine $H$: substitute $z = T - t$. In particular, $z = -t$ gives $H(0)$.
  • Example: $H(T) = T^2 - 4T + 5$ at $t = 1$ gives $H(1 + z) = 2 - 2z + z^2$, so $H(0) = 2 + 2 + 1 = 5.$

So one cheap point would do, given $3nR + 1$ Taylor coefficients there. But each extra coefficient costs time.

Few points, more data

Combine the two: at each of the $Q$ points, compute the first $J$ Taylor coefficients of $H$.

Claim If $QJ > 3nR$, this data determines $H$.

Proof. Two polynomials of degree $\le 3nR$ with the same data differ by a multiple of $(T - t)^J$ for every $t$, hence of $\prod_{t=1}^{Q} (T - t)^J$, which has degree $QJ > 3nR$. So they are equal. $\blacksquare$

More Taylor coefficients cost more time. The total stays $2^{o(n)}$ when $J \approx 3nR/Q = o(n)$ — that is, when $R \ll Q$.

What it means

  • If the proof holds, ETH and SETH are false. Week 2's SETH lower bound for LCS and Week 11's “no $2^{o(n)}$ Subset Sum under ETH” lose their foundation.
  • It does not make every NP-complete problem $2^{o(N)}$: a reduction to 3‑SAT with $N^2$ variables gives only $2^{o(N^2)}.$
  • It does count independent sets and $q$-colorings of a graph on $n$ vertices in $2^{o(n)}$ time.

Part 1: Why Approximate?

Where Week 11 left us

Vertex cover, independent set, 3SAT, Hamiltonian path … are NP-complete. No polynomial-time exact algorithm unless P = NP.

But the inputs keep coming. What are our options?

  • Exact, exponential: become infeasible on large enough inputs.
  • Special structure: vertex cover is easy on trees (W3) and bipartite graphs (W8) — but our graph may be neither.

Where Week 11 left us

  • Heuristics: often good, but no guarantee — we can't tell a good answer from a terrible one.
  • Approximation algorithms: polynomial time, and a provable worst-case guarantee on solution quality.

Today: approximation algorithms. We trade exactness for speed — but keep a proof.

Approximately Optimal

Definition For a minimization problem, an algorithm is an $\alpha$-approximation ($\alpha \ge 1$) if it runs in polynomial time and, on every input, outputs a feasible solution of cost $\mathrm{ALG} \le \alpha \cdot \mathrm{OPT}$.
  • For maximization: $\mathrm{ALG} \ge \mathrm{OPT}/\alpha$ (so $\alpha \ge 1$ in both conventions).
  • Worst case, per instance — not on average, not “usually”. A 2-approximation for vertex cover never returns a cover more than twice the smallest.
  • Smaller $\alpha$ is better; $\alpha = 1$ means exact.

The one trick behind every proof today

To prove $\mathrm{ALG} \le \alpha \cdot \mathrm{OPT}$ we would seem to need $\mathrm{OPT}$ — the very number that is NP-hard to compute.

Key idea Compare against a lower bound instead. Find an efficiently analyzable quantity $\mathrm{LB} \le \mathrm{OPT}$ and show $\mathrm{ALG} \le \alpha \cdot \mathrm{LB}$. Then $$\mathrm{ALG} \;\le\; \alpha \cdot \mathrm{LB} \;\le\; \alpha \cdot \mathrm{OPT}.$$

The One Trick

Every algorithm today is really a pair: a solution, and a lower bound certifying its quality.

Part 2: Vertex Cover via Maximal Matching

Recall: covers and matchings

Definition A vertex cover of $G=(V,E)$ is a set $C \subseteq V$ touching every edge: for all $\{u,v\} \in E$, $u \in C$ or $v \in C$.
Definition A matching $M$ is maximal if no edge of $G$ can be added to it: every edge of $G$ shares an endpoint with some edge of $M$.

Recall: covers and matchings

Careful Maximal $\ne$ maximum. A maximal matching is merely unextendable; a maximum matching has the largest possible size. Greedy (W4 flavour) gives a maximal matching in $O(m)$ time — no augmenting paths, no flows needed.

The algorithm (Gavril / Yannakakis)

function MATCHING-VC(G = (V, E)):
    M ← ∅
    for each edge {u, v} ∈ E:            // any order
        if u unmatched and v unmatched:
            add {u, v} to M              // M stays a matching
    return C = { every endpoint of every edge in M }
  • One pass over the edges: $O(m)$ time.
  • $M$ is maximal by construction: any skipped edge had a matched endpoint at the time — and vertices never become unmatched.
  • Output size: $|C| = 2|M|$.

Two claims to prove: $C$ is a cover, and $|C| \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$.

Worked example

Correctness: $C$ covers every edge

  • Suppose some edge $\{u,v\}$ has neither endpoint in $C$.
  • Then neither $u$ nor $v$ is an endpoint of any matching edge — both are unmatched.
  • So $M \cup \{\{u,v\}\}$ is still a matching — contradicting maximality of $M$.
  • Hence every edge is covered: $C$ is a vertex cover. $\blacksquare$

The factor of 2

  • The edges of $M$ are pairwise disjoint (it's a matching).
  • Any vertex cover — including an optimal one — must cover each edge of $M$, and no single vertex covers two of them.
  • So every vertex cover uses $\ge |M|$ distinct vertices: $\;\mathrm{OPT} \ge |M|$.
  • Therefore $$|C| \;=\; 2|M| \;\le\; 2\,\mathrm{OPT}.$$

The factor of 2

Theorem MATCHING-VC is a 2-approximation for (unweighted) vertex cover, in $O(m)$ time.

Remarks on the matching algorithm

  • The analysis is tight. On $K_{n,n}$ the algorithm finds a perfect matching and outputs all $2n$ vertices, while one side ($n$ vertices) is an optimal cover. Ratio $\to 2$.
  • Simplicity is a feature: no LP, no flow, one pass.

Remarks on the matching algorithm

  • Limitations: it ignores vertex weights — both endpoints go in, however expensive. Weighted VC needs the next two methods.

State of the art: $2 - \Theta(1/\sqrt{\log n})$ (Karakostas 2005). Beating $2 - \varepsilon$ is NP-hard (Khot–Regev 2008, given the 2026 OpenAI proof of the Unique Games Conjecture). So factor 2 is essentially the best possible.

Part 3: Vertex Cover via LP Rounding

LP rounding (recall Week 9)

Weighted vertex cover, relaxed: $\ \min \sum_v w_v x_v$ s.t. $x_u + x_v \ge 1$ on every edge, $x \ge 0$.

function LP-ROUND-VC(G, w):
    solve the LP relaxation  →  optimal x*      // poly time (W9)
    return C = { v : x*_v ≥ 1/2 }               // round halves up
Week 9 proved $C$ is a cover (every edge has an endpoint with $x^* \ge \tfrac12$), and $w(C) \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}$ (each $v \in C$ has $2x^*_v \ge 1$).

LP rounding as an algorithm

Theorem LP rounding is a 2-approximation for weighted vertex cover: $\ w(C) \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}_{LP} \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$.
  • Week 9 read this as a fact about the LP: its integrality gap is at most $2$. Today it is an algorithm — polynomial time, with a guarantee.

LP rounding as an algorithm

  • It even certifies itself: $\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}$ is a lower bound computed along the way, so $w(C) / \mathrm{OPT}_{LP}$ bounds this run's actual ratio.
  • Same factor as the matching algorithm, but now with weights: the LP weighs each vertex's cost when choosing which endpoints to cover.

Why 2 is a wall

  • Week 9: on $K_n$ the LP takes every $x_v = \tfrac12$, value $\tfrac{n}{2}$, while any cover needs $n - 1$ vertices. The gap is $2 - \tfrac{2}{n}$.
  • Any proof of the form $\mathrm{ALG} \le \alpha\,\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}$ forces $\alpha \ge$ integrality gap. So this lower bound cannot certify better than $\approx 2$.
  • And not just for LPs: beating $2 - \varepsilon$ is NP-hard (Khot–Regev), so no polynomial-time algorithm gets past 2 unless P = NP.

Part 4: Vertex Cover, Primal–Dual

Can we have both?

  • Maximal matching: no LP — but ignores weights.
  • LP rounding: handles weights — but solves an LP first.

Both analyses charge the cover to a lower bound on $\mathrm{OPT}$: a matching, or $\mathrm{OPT}_{LP}$. Only the second needs a solver.

Week 10 offers cheaper lower bounds: any feasible dual solution. Plan: build a dual solution greedily, and pay for the cover out of it.

The dual: packing edges (W10 recipe)

Applying the dualization recipe from Week 10 to the VC relaxation (one dual variable $y_e \ge 0$ per edge constraint):

Primal (fractional cover)

$$\begin{aligned} \min\; & \textstyle\sum_v w_v x_v \\ \text{s.t. } & x_u + x_v \ge 1 \;\; \forall \{u,v\} \\ & x \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Dual (fractional edge packing)

$$\begin{aligned} \max\; & \textstyle\sum_e y_e \\ \text{s.t. } & \textstyle\sum_{e \,\ni\, v} y_e \le w_v \;\; \forall v \\ & y \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

The dual: packing edges (W10 recipe)

  • Interpretation: put money $y_e$ on each edge, never loading any vertex beyond its price $w_v$ — a fractional matching. (Bipartite + unit weights: exactly the Kőnig matching/cover pair of W10.)
  • Weak duality (W10): every feasible $y$ satisfies $\sum_e y_e \le \mathrm{OPT}_{LP} \le \mathrm{OPT}$. Any feasible dual is a lower bound — no need to solve either LP to optimality!

The primal–dual idea

Complementary slackness (W10) says: at optimality, $x_v > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sum_{e \ni v} y_e = w_v$ — you only buy vertices whose dual constraint is tight.

Primal–dual algorithms take this as a design rule rather than an optimality condition.

The primal–dual idea

The design rule, in three steps:

  • Grow a feasible dual $y$ from $0$ (our lower bound grows with it).
  • Only add vertex $v$ to the cover when its constraint becomes tight: $\sum_{e \ni v} y_e = w_v$. Its cost is then paid for by the dual money on its edges.
  • Keep raising duals on edges that are still uncovered, until none remain.

Primal–dual algorithm

function PRIMAL-DUAL-VC(G, w):
    y_e ← 0 for all e;   C ← ∅
    slack(v) ← w_v for all v          // slack(v) = w_v − Σ_{e∋v} y_e
    while some edge {u, v} has u ∉ C and v ∉ C:
        δ ← min(slack(u), slack(v))
        y_uv ← y_uv + δ                 // raise until an endpoint is tight
        slack(u) ← slack(u) − δ;  slack(v) ← slack(v) − δ
        add every endpoint with slack 0 to C   // tight ⇒ bought
    return C
  • $y$ stays dual-feasible: we never raise $y_{uv}$ past the smaller remaining slack.
  • Each iteration covers its edge (one endpoint goes tight), so at most $m$ iterations: $O(m)$ arithmetic. No LP solver anywhere.
  • Termination condition is feasibility: the loop exits only when every edge has an endpoint in $C$.

Primal–dual: the factor of 2

  • Every $v \in C$ was added when tight: $\;w_v = \sum_{e \ni v} y_e$.
  • Sum over the cover: $$w(C) \;=\; \sum_{v \in C} \sum_{e \ni v} y_e \;\le\; 2 \sum_{e \in E} y_e,$$ since each edge $e$ has only 2 endpoints, so $y_e$ is counted at most twice.
  • $y$ is dual-feasible, so by weak duality $\;\sum_e y_e \le \mathrm{OPT}_{LP} \le \mathrm{OPT}$.
  • Chain them: $\;w(C) \le 2\sum_e y_e \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$. $\blacksquare$

Primal–dual: the factor of 2

Theorem PRIMAL-DUAL-VC is a 2-approximation for weighted vertex cover in $O(m)$ time — LP-quality guarantee, no LP solved.

Three 2-approximations, one problem

  • Cute fact: with unit weights, primal–dual is the matching algorithm. Raising $y_{uv}$ to $1$ makes both endpoints tight at once, so tight vertices = matched endpoints, and the raised edges form a maximal matching.
  • Same factor three ways is no accident: all three proofs bound the output against (something at most) the LP optimum, and the integrality gap of that LP is $2 - o(1)$.

Part 5: Metric TSP via MST

The travelling salesperson problem

Problem Given costs $c(u,v)$ on all pairs from a set of $n$ cities, find a minimum-cost tour: a cycle visiting every city exactly once.

TSP is NP-hard (its decision version contains Hamiltonian cycle, W11). Worse: approximating general TSP is hopeless.

The travelling salesperson problem

Careful For arbitrary costs, no $\alpha$-approximation exists for any constant $\alpha$ unless P = NP. Given a graph $G$, set $c(u,v) = 1$ on edges of $G$ and $c(u,v) = \alpha n + 1$ off them: an $\alpha$-approximation returns a tour of cost $\le \alpha n$ iff $G$ has a Hamiltonian cycle — deciding an NP-complete problem.

So we need an honest extra assumption on the costs.

Metric TSP

Assumption The costs form a metric: $c(u,v) = c(v,u) \ge 0$ and the triangle inequality holds: $c(u,w) \le c(u,v) + c(v,w)$ for all $u,v,w$.
  • “Direct is never worse than via a detour.” Points in the plane, road networks, shortest-path distances in any graph: all metrics.
  • Metric TSP is still NP-hard — but now approximable.
  • Our lower bound will come from an old friend: the minimum spanning tree (Weeks 5–6).

The lower bound: $\mathrm{MST} \le \mathrm{OPT}$

  • Take an optimal tour, cost $\mathrm{OPT}$.
  • Delete any one edge of it.
  • What's left is a spanning path — in particular a spanning tree.
  • Its cost is $\le \mathrm{OPT}$ (we removed a nonnegative-cost edge), and the MST costs no more: $$c(\mathrm{MST}) \;\le\; \mathrm{OPT}.$$

A tree we can compute exactly in $O(m \log n)$ (Kruskal, Prim) lower-bounds a quantity that is NP-hard to compute. That asymmetry powers the algorithm.

The double-tree algorithm

function DOUBLE-TREE-TSP(cities, metric c):
    T ← MST of the complete graph on the cities   // Kruskal / Prim, W5–6
    D ← T with every edge doubled                 // every degree is now even
    W ← Euler tour of D                           // connected + even degrees ⇒ exists
    H ← shortcut W: follow W, skip any city already visited
    return H                                       // a cycle through every city once
  • Euler tour: a closed walk using every edge of $D$ exactly once — it exists precisely because doubling made all degrees even, and it visits every city (possibly several times).
  • Shortcutting: whenever the walk would revisit a city, jump directly to the next new one — legal because the graph is complete, and cheap because of the triangle inequality.

Step 1: the cities and their MST

Step 2: double every edge

Step 3: shortcut the Euler tour

Metric TSP: the factor of 2

  • The Euler tour $W$ uses each edge of the doubled tree once: $$c(W) \;=\; 2\, c(\mathrm{MST}).$$
  • Shortcutting replaces a stretch $u \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_k \to v$ of $W$ by the single edge $uv$; by (repeated) triangle inequality this never increases the cost: $$c(H) \;\le\; c(W).$$

Metric TSP: the factor of 2

  • And the MST is our lower bound: $c(\mathrm{MST}) \le \mathrm{OPT}$.
  • Chain: $\;c(H) \le 2\,c(\mathrm{MST}) \le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$. $\blacksquare$
Theorem Double-tree is a 2-approximation for metric TSP.

Can do better: replace “double the tree” by “add a min-cost perfect matching on the odd-degree vertices” — Christofides (1976) gets factor $\tfrac32$; only in 2021 was $\tfrac32 - 10^{-36}$ achieved (Karlin–Klein–Oveis Gharan).

Part 6: Greedy Set Cover

Set cover

Problem Given a universe $U$ of $n$ elements and sets $S_1, \dots, S_m \subseteq U$ with $\bigcup_i S_i = U$, choose the fewest sets whose union is $U$.

Set cover

  • Vertex cover is the special case: elements = edges, one set per vertex (its incident edges) — each element in exactly 2 sets. So set cover is NP-hard too.
  • Real face of the problem: choosing test suites, sensor placement, crew scheduling, feature selection …

The greedy algorithm

function GREEDY-SET-COVER(U, S_1..S_m):
    R ← U                              // still uncovered
    picked ← ∅
    while R ≠ ∅:
        S ← a set maximizing |S ∩ R|    // covers the most uncovered
        picked ← picked ∪ {S};   R ← R \ S
    return picked

The obvious algorithm — grab whatever helps most right now. Runs in polynomial time. The question is how much it can lose.

The greedy algorithm

Theorem Greedy uses at most $H_n \cdot \mathrm{OPT}$ sets, where $H_n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac1n \le \ln n + 1$.

A $(\ln n + 1)$-approximation — our first non-constant factor. We prove it by charging the cost to elements.

Charging, step 1: every element pays a price

  • When greedy picks a set $S$ that covers $t$ new elements, spread its cost over them: each of the $t$ elements is charged $\mathrm{price}(e) = 1/t$.
  • Every picked set distributes exactly $1$ unit, and every element is charged exactly once (when first covered), so $$\#\{\text{sets greedy picks}\} \;=\; \sum_{e \in U} \mathrm{price}(e).$$

Example: prices

  • Greedy picks $S_1$ first: 4 new elements (each row has 3). So $a, b, d, e$ pay $\tfrac14$ each.
  • Then $S_2$: only $c$ is new ($a$ and $b$ have already paid), so $c$ pays $1$. Then $S_3$: $f$ pays $1$.
  • Total: $4 \cdot \tfrac14 + 1 + 1 = 3$, the number of sets greedy picked. But $\mathrm{OPT} = 2$: the two rows $S_2, S_3$.

Charging, step 1: every element pays a price

Plan: bound each element's price, then sum.

Prices are highest late in the run, when picked sets cover only a few new elements each.

Charging, step 2: the key lemma

Lemma Consider the moment when $k$ elements are still uncovered. Then every element covered by greedy's next pick is charged at most $\mathrm{OPT}/k$.
  • The optimal solution's $\mathrm{OPT}$ sets cover all of $U$ — in particular all $k$ remaining elements.
  • By pigeonhole, some single set covers $\ge k/\mathrm{OPT}$ of them. (This is today's lower-bound trick, inverted: OPT's mere existence promises a good set.)

Charging, step 2: the key lemma

  • Greedy picks a set covering the most uncovered elements, so its pick covers $t \ge k/\mathrm{OPT}$ new elements.
  • Each of them pays $1/t \le \mathrm{OPT}/k$. $\blacksquare$

Example: bounding the prices

Number the elements by when greedy covers them, last first. Each set is drawn over the elements it newly covers. Here $\mathrm{OPT} = 2$.

The slack: elements 3–6 were all covered when 6 remained, so each pays at most $2/6$. Yet $\mathrm{OPT}/k$ charges element $k$ as if only $k$ remained.

Charging, step 3: sum the harmonic series

Order elements by when greedy covers them, last first. The $k$-th from last pays at most $\mathrm{OPT}/k$, so:

$$\begin{aligned} \#\text{sets} \;&=\; \sum_{e} \mathrm{price}(e) \;\le\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{\mathrm{OPT}}{k}\\ &=\; H_n \cdot \mathrm{OPT} \;\le\; (\ln n + 1)\,\mathrm{OPT}. \;\blacksquare \end{aligned}$$

Charging, step 3: sum the harmonic series

  • The last element covered is the expensive one ($k=1$: price up to $\mathrm{OPT}$); early elements are nearly free. The harmonic sum totals the damage.
  • The same proof handles weighted set cover (pick the set minimizing cost per new element): factor $H_n$ again.
  • Applied to vertex cover it gives $O(\log n)$ — worse than our factor 2! General-purpose tools pay for their generality.

Greedy really can lose a log factor

Scale to $2 \times 2^k$ with block sizes $2^k, 2^{k-1}, \dots$ (perturb sizes to force the ties): greedy uses $\approx \log_2 n$ sets while $\mathrm{OPT} = 2$. The $H_n$ analysis is essentially tight.

And nothing beats greedy (probably)

Theorem For every $\varepsilon > 0$, a $(1-\varepsilon)\ln n$-approximation for set cover would imply P = NP.

Dinur & Steurer 2014, completing a line started by Lund–Yannakakis 1994 and Feige 1998.

  • So the most naive algorithm imaginable is optimal to within the constant in front of $\ln n$.

And nothing beats greedy (probably)

  • Moral of the week: approximability is a spectrum. VC and metric TSP sit at constant factor; set cover at $\Theta(\log n)$; general TSP at “nothing, ever”. NP-completeness (W11) collapses all of these to “hard” — approximation reveals the finer structure.
