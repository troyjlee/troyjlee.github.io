Goal A short, checkable certificate of optimality for any LP. Today's answer: a solution to a second LP — the dual.

But checking all vertices is not a certificate that scales. We want an upper bound proof.

Bounding from Above

$$\begin{gathered}\max\ 3x_1 + 2x_2 \quad\text{s.t.}\\ \underbrace{x_1 \le 6}_{\text{machine}},\ \ \underbrace{x_1 + x_2 \le 8}_{\text{wood}},\ \ \underbrace{x_1 + 3x_2 \le 18}_{\text{varnish}},\ \ x_1, x_2 \ge 0\end{gathered}$$

Idea: combine the constraints. Multiply each by a number $y_i \ge 0$ and add. If the result dominates the objective coefficient-wise, its right-hand side is an upper bound.

Attempt 1 — take $2\times$ (machine) $+\ 1\times$ (varnish): $$2x_1 + (x_1 + 3x_2) \;=\; 3x_1 + 3x_2 \;\le\; 12 + 18 \;=\; 30.$$

Since $x_2 \ge 0$:  $3x_1 + 2x_2 \le 3x_1 + 3x_2 \le 30$.  Bound: $30$.

The perfect combination

Attempt 3 — take $1\times$ (machine) $+\ 2\times$ (wood):

$$x_1 + 2(x_1 + x_2) \;=\; 3x_1 + 2x_2 \;\le\; 6 + 16 \;=\; 22.$$

This time the combination is exactly the objective — no slack wasted. Bound: $22$.

And the feasible point $(6,2)$ achieves $22$.

Certified Every feasible point has value $\le 22$, and $(6,2)$ has value $22$. The pair — point $(6,2)$, multipliers $(y_1,y_2,y_3)=(1,2,0)$ — is a two-line proof of optimality anyone can check.

The best bound is an LP

Three constraints $\to$ three dual variables; two primal variables $\to$ two dual constraints.

The best bound is an LP

Definition The search for the best upper bound is itself a linear program: the dual of the original (primal) LP.

Part II — The Dual Recipe

Running example, in matrices

$$\begin{gathered}\max\ 3x_1 + 2x_2 \quad\text{s.t.}\\ \underbrace{x_1 \le 6}_{\text{machine}},\ \ \underbrace{x_1 + x_2 \le 8}_{\text{wood}},\ \ \underbrace{x_1 + 3x_2 \le 18}_{\text{varnish}},\ \ x_1, x_2 \ge 0\end{gathered}$$

The same LP in matrix form:

$$\begin{gathered} \max\ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}}_{c^{\mathsf T}} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}}_{x} \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}}_{A} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} \le \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 8 \\ 18 \end{pmatrix}}_{b},\ \ x \ge 0 \end{gathered}$$

Combining constraints

Multiply the constraints by $y_1, y_2, y_3 \ge 0$ and add. The left side is

$$\begin{pmatrix} y_1 & y_2 & y_3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = (y_1 + y_2 + y_3)\, x_1 + (y_2 + 3y_3)\, x_2.$$

Combining constraints

We want the combination to dominate $3x_1 + 2x_2$, coefficient by coefficient:

$$\begin{pmatrix} y_1 & y_2 & y_3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \ge \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad y^{\mathsf T} A \ge c^{\mathsf T}.$$

Multiplied out: $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 \ge 3$ and $y_2 + 3y_3 \ge 2$ — the dual constraints from Part I.

The bound, in one line

Claim If $y \ge 0$ and $y^{\mathsf T} A \ge c^{\mathsf T}$, then every feasible $x$ satisfies $$c^{\mathsf T} x \;\le\; y^{\mathsf T} A x \;\le\; y^{\mathsf T} b.$$
  • First $\le$: $y^{\mathsf T} A$ dominates $c^{\mathsf T}$ entrywise, and $x \ge 0$.
  • Second $\le$: $Ax \le b$, weighted by $y \ge 0$.
  • Attempt 3 was $y = (1, 2, 0)$:   $y^{\mathsf T} A = (3, 2) = c^{\mathsf T}$,   $y^{\mathsf T} b = 6 + 16 = 22$.

Dual of the standard form

Nothing used the specific numbers. Any LP in Week 9's standard form ($A \in \R^{m\times n}$) $$\max\ c^{\mathsf T} x \quad \text{s.t.}\quad Ax \le b,\ \ x \ge 0$$ has the same bound: $c^{\mathsf T} x \le y^{\mathsf T} b$ for every $y \in \R^m$ with $y \ge 0$ and $y^{\mathsf T} A \ge c^{\mathsf T}$.

Transposing, $y^{\mathsf T} A \ge c^{\mathsf T}$ is the same as $A^{\mathsf T} y \ge c$: one constraint per column of $A$.

Dual of the standard form

Best bound = smallest right-hand side:

Dual LP $$\min\ b^{\mathsf T} y \quad \text{s.t.}\quad A^{\mathsf T} y \ge c,\ \ y \ge 0.$$ Constraints of the primal $\leftrightarrow$ variables of the dual, and vice versa; $A$ becomes $A^{\mathsf T}$; $b$ and $c$ trade places.

Mixed constraints

Real LPs mix $\le$ rows and $=$ rows:

$$\begin{aligned} \max\ \ & 3x_1 + x_2\\ \text{s.t.}\ \ & x_1 + x_2 = 4\\ & x_1 - x_2 \le 2\\ & x_1, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Combining constraints still works, but the signs need care: may the equality get a negative multiplier?

One idea answers this: penalise, don't enforce.

Penalise, don't enforce

Keep the sign constraints $x_1, x_2 \ge 0$. Drop the other constraints and add a term per row to the objective, with a price $y_i$:

$$L(x,y) = 3x_1 + x_2 + y_1\,(4 - x_1 - x_2) + y_2\,(2 - x_1 + x_2).$$
Goal $L$ never undercuts the objective on feasible points: $$3x_1 + x_2 \;\le\; L(x,y) \quad\text{for every feasible } x.$$ Then $\max_x L(x,y)$ bounds OPT from above. Choose the signs of $y$ to make this hold.

Penalise, don't enforce

$$L(x,y) = 3x_1 + x_2 + y_1\,(4 - x_1 - x_2) + y_2\,(2 - x_1 + x_2)$$

Goal: every added term is $\ge 0$ on feasible points.

  • Feasible $x$ has $4 - x_1 - x_2 = 0$:   the term vanishes, so $y_1$ may have any sign.
  • Feasible $x$ has $2 - x_1 + x_2 \ge 0$:   we need $y_2 \ge 0$.
  • Now a violated $\le$ row ($x_1 - x_2 > 2$) makes its term negative. We are maximising, so that is a penalty: $y_2$ per unit of violation.

Every $y$ gives a bound

For $y_1$ free and $y_2 \ge 0$: $$\text{OPT} \;\le\; \max_{x_1, x_2 \ge 0} L(x,y).$$

The max on the right is over all such $x$, feasible or not — that only makes it larger.

Regroup $L$ by variable:

$$\begin{aligned} L(x,y) &= 3x_1 + x_2 + y_1\,(4 - x_1 - x_2) + y_2\,(2 - x_1 + x_2)\\ &= 4y_1 + 2y_2 + (3 - y_1 - y_2)\,x_1 + (1 - y_1 + y_2)\,x_2. \end{aligned}$$

When is the bound finite?

$$L(x,y) = 4y_1 + 2y_2 + (3 - y_1 - y_2)\,x_1 + (1 - y_1 + y_2)\,x_2$$

A bound of $+\infty$ is useless. Which $y$ keep $\max_x L(x,y)$ finite?

  • A positive coefficient on $x_1$ lets $x_1 \to \infty$.   Need $y_1 + y_2 \ge 3$.
  • Likewise for $x_2$.   Need $y_1 - y_2 \ge 1$.
  • With both, each $x_j$ term is $\le 0$ for every $x_j \ge 0$ — largest at $x = 0$. So $$\max_{x_1, x_2 \ge 0} L(x,y) = 4y_1 + 2y_2.$$

The best bound

Fix a $y$ with $y_2 \ge 0$ that satisfies the constraints from the previous slide. Then for every feasible $x$:

$$3x_1 + x_2 \;\le\; L(x,y) \;\le\; \max_{x'} L(x',y) \;=\; 4y_1 + 2y_2.$$

Every such $y$ bounds OPT. The best bound is the smallest:

Dual — derived, not looked up
$$\begin{gathered}\min\ 4y_1 + 2y_2 \quad\text{s.t.}\\ y_1 + y_2 \ge 3,\ \ y_1 - y_2 \ge 1,\ \ y_1 \text{ free},\ y_2 \ge 0\end{gathered}$$

The Lagrangian

General primal: $\max\ c^{\mathsf T}x$ subject to rows $a_i^{\mathsf T}x \le b_i$ or $a_i^{\mathsf T}x = b_i$, and $x \ge 0$. The Lagrangian adds price $y_i$ times the slack of row $i$:

$$L(x,y) = c^{\mathsf T}x + \textstyle\sum_i y_i\,(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x) = c^{\mathsf T}x + y^{\mathsf T}(b - Ax).$$

Goal: $c^{\mathsf T}x \le L(x,y)$ for every feasible $x$, i.e. every term $y_i\,(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x) \ge 0$:

  • $a_i^{\mathsf T}x \le b_i$:   the slack is $\ge 0$, so $y_i \ge 0$.
  • $a_i^{\mathsf T}x = b_i$:   the slack is $0$, so $y_i$ is unrestricted.

The Lagrangian

Regroup by variable:

$$L(x,y) = b^{\mathsf T}y + (c - A^{\mathsf T}y)^{\mathsf T}x = b^{\mathsf T}y + \textstyle\sum_j \big(c_j - (A^{\mathsf T}y)_j\big)\, x_j.$$

The max over $x$ is finite exactly when no $x_j$ can push $L$ to $+\infty$:

  • Since $x_j \ge 0$, its coefficient must be $\le 0$:   $(A^{\mathsf T}y)_j \ge c_j$, i.e. $A^{\mathsf T}y \ge c$.
  • Then every $x_j$ term is $\le 0$, the max is at $x = 0$, and it equals $b^{\mathsf T}y$.
  • Best bound: minimise $b^{\mathsf T}y$ over these $y$ — the dual.

Sanity check: standard form ($Ax \le b$, $x \ge 0$) gives $\min\ b^{\mathsf T}y$ s.t. $A^{\mathsf T}y \ge c$, $y \ge 0$ — the dual from before.

Do the bounds meet?

Primal

$$\begin{aligned} \max\ \ & 3x_1 + x_2\\ & x_1 + x_2 = 4\\ & x_1 - x_2 \le 2\\ & x_1, x_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Dual

$$\begin{aligned} \min\ \ & 4y_1 + 2y_2\\ & y_1 + y_2 \ge 3\\ & y_1 - y_2 \ge 1\\ & y_1\ \text{free},\ y_2 \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Check with numbers. Primal: $x_2 = 4 - x_1$ and $x_1 - x_2 \le 2$ give $x_1 \le 3$; optimum $x^\star = (3,1)$, value $\mathbf{10}$.

Dual: $y^\star = (2,1)$ is feasible ($3 \ge 3$, $1 \ge 1$), value $8 + 2 = \mathbf{10}$. They meet!

Dual of the dual

Fact For standard form, the dual of the dual is the primal: negate the dual to get a standard-form max, take its dual, negate back.
$$\begin{aligned} &\min\{b^{\mathsf T}y : A^{\mathsf T}y \ge c,\ y \ge 0\}\\ &= -\max\{-b^{\mathsf T}y : -A^{\mathsf T}y \le -c,\ y \ge 0\}\\ &\xrightarrow{\text{dual}}\ -\min\{-c^{\mathsf T}x : -Ax \ge -b,\ x \ge 0\}\\ &= \max\{c^{\mathsf T}x : Ax \le b,\ x \ge 0\}. \end{aligned}$$

Dual of the dual

Consequences:

  • Duality is a genuine pairing, not a one-way construction — each LP certifies the other.
  • "Primal" and "dual" are roles, not properties: solve or analyse whichever of the pair is more convenient.

Part III — Weak and Strong Duality

Weak duality

Theorem (weak duality) If $x$ is feasible for the primal $\max\{c^{\mathsf T}x : Ax \le b,\ x\ge 0\}$ and $y$ is feasible for its dual, then $\;c^{\mathsf T}x \le b^{\mathsf T}y$.

Proof (one line): $$c^{\mathsf T} x \;\le\; (A^{\mathsf T} y)^{\mathsf T} x \;=\; y^{\mathsf T}(Ax) \;\le\; y^{\mathsf T} b. \qquad \blacksquare$$

Notice: the proof is exactly the "combine the constraints" trick from Part I, written once and for all.

Weak duality

Unpacking each step of $\;c^{\mathsf T} x \le (A^{\mathsf T} y)^{\mathsf T} x = y^{\mathsf T}(Ax) \le y^{\mathsf T} b$:

  • First $\le$: $A^{\mathsf T}y \ge c$ coefficient-wise (dual feasibility), and $x \ge 0$ — multiplying a bigger coefficient by a nonnegative number can only increase the sum.
  • Middle $=$: just re-bracketing, $(A^{\mathsf T}y)^{\mathsf T}x = y^{\mathsf T}Ax = y^{\mathsf T}(Ax)$.
  • Second $\le$: $Ax \le b$ row-wise (primal feasibility), and $y \ge 0$.

Certificates for Free

Corollary If $x^\star$ is primal feasible, $y^\star$ is dual feasible, and $c^{\mathsf T}x^\star = b^{\mathsf T}y^\star$, then both are optimal.

Every primal value sits below every dual value — if they touch, neither side can move.

This is the same logic as Week 8's worksheet: a flow of value $v$ plus a cut of capacity $v$ certify each other.

Strong duality

Theorem (strong duality — stated, not proved) If the primal has a finite optimum, so does the dual, and the two optimal values are equal: $$\begin{gathered} \max\{c^{\mathsf T}x : Ax \le b,\ x \ge 0\} \\ {}=\; \min\{b^{\mathsf T}y : A^{\mathsf T}y \ge c,\ y \ge 0\}. \end{gathered}$$

So the meeting at $22$ was no accident: a perfect certificate always exists. The bound-combining game can always be played to a draw.

Strong duality as minimax

Assume the standard-form primal has a finite optimum. With $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$, $\;L(x,y) = c^{\mathsf T}x + y^{\mathsf T}(b - Ax).$

Convention: write $\min = -\infty$ or $\max = +\infty$ when the expression is unbounded below or above, respectively.

  • Fix $x$, let $y$ respond: a violated row lets $y_i \to \infty$, so $$\min_{y \ge 0} L(x,y) = \begin{cases} c^{\mathsf T}x & \text{if } Ax \le b,\\ -\infty & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ Hence $\max_{x}\min_{y} L$ is the primal optimum.
  • Fix $y$, let $x$ respond: $\max_{x \ge 0} L(x,y) = b^{\mathsf T}y$ if $A^{\mathsf T}y \ge c$, else $+\infty$ (Part II). Hence $\min_{y}\max_{x} L$ is the dual optimum.

Strong duality as minimax

Strong duality, restated If the primal has a finite optimum, then $$\max_{x \ge 0}\ \min_{y \ge 0}\ L(x,y) \;=\; \min_{y \ge 0}\ \max_{x \ge 0}\ L(x,y).$$
  • $\le$ is automatic — that is weak duality:   $\min_{y} L(x',y) \le L(x',y') \le \max_{x} L(x,y')$ for every $x', y'$.
  • $=$ is the deep part (proof beyond this course). As a game: one player picks a production plan $x$, the other picks prices $y$. Strong duality says moving second gives no advantage.
  • Von Neumann's minimax theorem for zero-sum games is a special case of LP duality.

Part IV — Max-Flow / Min-Cut, Round Two

Recall Week 8

Theorem (max-flow = min-cut) In any $s$–$t$ network, the maximum flow value equals the minimum capacity of an $s$–$t$ cut.

Week 8's proof: run Edmonds–Karp (Ford–Fulkerson with BFS) until no augmenting path exists; let $S$ = vertices reachable from $s$ in the residual graph; the cut $(S, V\setminus S)$ is saturated, so its capacity equals the flow value.

Today we re-prove the same theorem without ever mentioning residual graphs — it will fall out of LP duality.

Max Flow as an LP

Digraph $G=(V,E)$, capacities $c_e \ge 0$, source $s$, sink $t$ (no edges into $s$). One variable $f_e$ per edge; $\delta^+(v)$ = edges out of $v$, $\delta^-(v)$ = edges into $v$:

$$\begin{aligned} \max\ \ & \textstyle\sum_{e \in \delta^+(s)} f_e \\ \text{s.t.}\ \ & f_e \le c_e \quad \forall e \in E\\ & \textstyle\sum_{e \in \delta^-(v)} f_e \;-\; \sum_{e \in \delta^+(v)} f_e = 0 \quad \forall v \ne s,t\\ & f_e \ge 0 \quad \forall e \in E \end{aligned}$$

A maximisation with $\le$ rows and $=$ rows. Penalise each row:

  • capacity constraint of edge $e$  $\to$  dual variable $y_e \ge 0$
  • conservation constraint at vertex $v$  $\to$  dual variable $z_v$, free (it's an equality)

Deriving the dual

Penalise every row, as in Part II:

$$\begin{aligned} L \;=\; & \textstyle\sum_{e \in \delta^+(s)} f_e \;+\; \sum_{e} y_e\,(c_e - f_e)\\ & {}+\; \textstyle\sum_{v \ne s,t} z_v \Big(\sum_{e \in \delta^+(v)} f_e - \sum_{e \in \delta^-(v)} f_e\Big). \end{aligned}$$

Then regroup by variable. Each $f_e$ gets one coefficient; since $f_e \ge 0$, a finite bound needs that coefficient $\le 0$. That is the dual constraint for edge $e$.

One edge at a time

Take an edge $e = (u, v)$. Every term of $L$ that contains $f_e$:

Coefficient $\le 0$:   $y_e \;\ge\; [u = s] + z_u - z_v$,   with no $z_s$ or $z_t$ term ($s$ and $t$ have no conservation row).

One edge at a time

Set $z_s := 1$ and $z_t := 0$. Then every edge, including those at $s$ and $t$, reads the same way:   $y_e \ge z_u - z_v$.

$$\begin{aligned} sa:\quad & y_{sa} \ge 1 - z_a\\ sb:\quad & y_{sb} \ge 1 - z_b\\ ab:\quad & y_{ab} \ge z_a - z_b\\ at:\quad & y_{at} \ge z_a\\ bt:\quad & y_{bt} \ge z_b \end{aligned}$$

Five edges, five dual constraints. What is left of $L$ is $\sum_e c_e\, y_e$: minimise it.

Deriving the dual

Dual LP $$\begin{aligned} \min\ \ & \textstyle\sum_{e} c_e\, y_e\\ \text{s.t.}\ \ & y_e \;\ge\; z_u - z_v \quad \forall e=(u,v) \in E\\ & z_s = 1,\quad z_t = 0,\quad y_e \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Intuition: pay $c_e$ per unit of $y_e$; $y_e$ must cover any drop in the label $z$ along edge $e$; $s$ is labelled $1$, $t$ is labelled $0$.

Reading the dual: it prices cuts

Try a $0/1$ solution: pick $S \subseteq V$ with $s \in S$, $t \notin S$, and set $z_v = 1$ if $v \in S$, else $0$.

Then $y_e \ge z_u - z_v$ forces $y_e \ge 1$ exactly on edges leaving $S$ — and $y_e = 0$ works everywhere else.

Dual objective $= \sum_{e\ \text{leaving}\ S} c_e$ = the capacity of the cut $S$.

Rounding to a cut

Every cut is a $0/1$ dual solution, so the LP optimum is at most the min cut. For the other direction, take any optimal dual $(y, z)$ and round it to a cut that costs no more.

  • Clip: raise each negative $z_v$ to $0$, lower each $z_v > 1$ to $1$.
    Still feasible: each new gap $z_u - z_v \le \max(0, \text{old gap}) \le y_e$.
  • Threshold: pick $\theta$ uniformly from $(0, 1]$ and let $$S_\theta = \{v : z_v \ge \theta\}.$$ It contains $s$ ($z_s = 1$) and never $t$ ($z_t = 0$): an $s$–$t$ cut.

Rounding to a cut

For the proof, return to an optimal dual $(y,z)$ after clipping.

  • Edge $e = (u, v)$ leaves $S_\theta$ exactly when $z_v < \theta \le z_u$: $$\Pr_{\theta}[e \text{ leaves } S_\theta] = \max(0,\, z_u - z_v) \;\le\; y_e.$$
  • Sum over edges, weighted by capacity: $$\begin{aligned}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\big[\text{cap}(S_\theta)\big] &= \textstyle\sum_e c_e \Pr_{\theta}[e \text{ leaves } S_\theta]\\ &\le \textstyle\sum_e c_e\, y_e \;=\; \text{LP optimum}.\end{aligned}$$
  • Some $\theta$ does at least as well as the average: a cut of capacity $\le$ LP optimum.

Rounding to a cut

Cuts bound the LP from above, and rounding finds a cut no bigger than it. So the dual LP optimum is the min cut.

Max-flow = min-cut
$$\text{max flow} \;\overset{\text{strong duality}}{=}\; \text{dual LP optimum} \;\overset{\text{rounding}}{=}\; \text{min cut}.$$

The question left hanging since Week 8: when we found a maximum bipartite matching, what certified that we could stop?

Deriving the cover dual

Graph $G=(V,E)$; variable $x_e$ per edge (how much of $e$ we take). The matching LP:

$$\max\ \textstyle\sum_{e \in E} x_e \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_{e \ni v} x_e \le 1 \ \ \forall v \in V,\quad x \ge 0.$$

Penalise each vertex row with a price $y_v$. It is a $\le$ row, so $y_v \ge 0$:

$$L = \textstyle\sum_{e} x_e \;+\; \sum_{v} y_v \Big(1 - \sum_{e \ni v} x_e\Big).$$

Then regroup by variable: the coefficient of each $x_e$ must be $\le 0$.

One edge at a time

Take an edge $e = uv$. Every term of $L$ that contains $x_e$:

Where $x_e$ appearsAdds to its coefficient
objective$+1$
vertex row of $u$$-y_u$
vertex row of $v$$-y_v$
any other vertex rownothing: $e$ doesn't touch it

Coefficient $1 - y_u - y_v \le 0$:   $y_u + y_v \ge 1$.
What is left of $L$ is $\sum_v y_v$: minimise it.

One edge at a time

Week 9's triangle: three vertex rows give $y_a, y_b, y_c \ge 0$; three edges give three dual constraints.

$$\begin{aligned} \min\ \ & y_a + y_b + y_c\\ ab:\quad & y_a + y_b \ge 1\\ ac:\quad & y_a + y_c \ge 1\\ bc:\quad & y_b + y_c \ge 1 \end{aligned}$$

Each edge must be covered by its two endpoints: a fractional vertex cover.

Matching $\leftrightarrow$ vertex cover

Matching LP (primal)

$$\begin{aligned} \max\ \ & \textstyle\sum_{e \in E} x_e\\ & \textstyle\sum_{e \ni v} x_e \le 1 \quad \forall v \in V\\ & x_e \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

Its dual (derived)

$$\begin{aligned} \min\ \ & \textstyle\sum_{v \in V} y_v\\ & y_u + y_v \ge 1 \quad \forall uv \in E\\ & y_v \ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

The dual is the fractional vertex cover LP: pay for vertices so that every edge is covered at least once. The two problems we kept meeting are LP duals.

Weak duality, combinatorially

Observation For any matching $M$ and any vertex cover $C$ in any graph: $\;|M| \le |C|$.

Why: each edge of $M$ must contain at least one vertex of $C$; and no vertex of $C$ can account for two edges of $M$, since matching edges are disjoint. So $C$ has at least one private vertex per matching edge. $\blacksquare$

This is exactly the weak duality inequality $c^{\mathsf T}x \le b^{\mathsf T}y$, specialised to $0/1$ solutions of the two LPs.

Weak duality, combinatorially

So: a matching and a cover of the same size would certify each other. Do they always exist? In general graphs, no (triangle: max matching $1$, min cover $2$). But…

Bipartite: the Gap Closes

Fact (integrality — Week 9) If $G$ is bipartite, the matching LP and the vertex cover LP both have optimal solutions that are $0/1$-valued.

(Week 9: the incidence matrix of a bipartite graph is totally unimodular, so by Cramer's rule every polytope vertex is integral.)

Bipartite: the Gap Closes

Recall Week 9's warning example: on the triangle $C_3$, setting $x_e = \tfrac12$ on all three edges gives matching-LP value $\tfrac32 > 1$ — an integrality gap. Odd cycles are the culprit, and bipartite graphs have none.

Now chain the equalities:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{max matching} &\overset{\text{integrality}}{=} \text{LP}_{\text{match}} \\ &\overset{\text{strong duality}}{=} \text{LP}_{\text{cover}} \\ &\overset{\text{integrality}}{=} \text{min vertex cover}. \end{aligned}$$

Kőnig's theorem

Theorem (Kőnig, 1931) In every bipartite graph, the size of a maximum matching equals the size of a minimum vertex cover.
  • The chain on the previous slide is the proof — duality plus integrality, no new combinatorics needed.
  • Practical upshot: a matching $M$ and a cover $C$ with $|M| = |C|$ form a mutual certificate — hand both to the skeptic.

The triangle

Which equalities from the bipartite chain survive on $K_3$?

Compute all four optima:

  1. Maximum matching
  2. Maximum fractional matching
  3. Minimum fractional cover
  4. Minimum vertex cover

The triangle: duality survives

$$\underbrace{1}_{\substack{\text{max}\\\text{matching}}} \;<\; \underbrace{\tfrac32}_{\text{matching LP}} \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac32}_{\text{cover LP}} \;<\; \underbrace{2}_{\substack{\text{min}\\\text{cover}}}.$$

Put $x_e=\tfrac12$ on every edge and $y_v=\tfrac12$ on every vertex. Both LP solutions are feasible and have value $\tfrac32$, so weak duality certifies that both are optimal.

Any two edges share a vertex, so a matching has size at most $1$. A vertex cover needs $2$ vertices.

What breaks? Strong duality still gives the middle equality. Both integrality equalities fail.

Worked example: a matching certifies a cover

Bipartite graph: edges $u_1v_1$, $u_1v_2$, $u_2v_1$, $u_3v_2$, $u_3v_3$, $u_4v_3$.

Matching $M = \{u_1v_2,\; u_2v_1,\; u_4v_3\}$, size $\mathbf{3}$ (only $u_3$ unmatched).

Cover $C = \{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$, size $\mathbf{3}$: every edge ends on the right, so all six are covered.

Worked example: a matching certifies a cover

Mutual certification Any cover needs $\ge |M| = 3$ vertices, so $C$ is a minimum cover; any matching has $\le |C| = 3$ edges, so $M$ is a maximum matching. Each is the other's proof.

Part VI — Complementary Slackness

Complementary slackness

When do a primal-feasible $x$ and dual-feasible $y$ actually meet, $c^{\mathsf T}x = b^{\mathsf T}y$?

Theorem (complementary slackness) Feasible $x$ and $y$ are both optimal if and only if:
  • for every $j$:   $x_j > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (A^{\mathsf T}y)_j = c_j$   (used variable $\Rightarrow$ its dual constraint is tight)
  • for every $i$:   $y_i > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (Ax)_i = b_i$   (paid-for constraint $\Rightarrow$ it is tight)

Complementary slackness

Slogan: at the optimum, nothing is both used and slack. You never pay a positive price $y_i$ for a constraint you aren't pressing against, and you never run a positive activity $x_j$ whose dual price exceeds its value.

Why it holds: squeeze the weak duality chain

Recall the one-line proof: $$c^{\mathsf T} x \;\overset{(1)}{\le}\; y^{\mathsf T} A x \;\overset{(2)}{\le}\; y^{\mathsf T} b.$$

If $x$ and $y$ are both optimal, strong duality says the two ends are equal — so $(1)$ and $(2)$ are equalities.

Why it holds: squeeze the weak duality chain

Both $\le$ in $\;c^{\mathsf T} x \overset{(1)}{\le} y^{\mathsf T} A x \overset{(2)}{\le} y^{\mathsf T} b\;$ are equalities at an optimal pair. Now read each termwise.

Inequality $(1)$ written termwise: $\;\sum_j x_j\big((A^{\mathsf T}y)_j - c_j\big) = 0$, a sum of nonnegative terms. A nonnegative sum is zero only if every term is zero: $x_j > 0$ forces $(A^{\mathsf T}y)_j = c_j$.

Same for $(2)$: $\;\sum_i y_i\big(b_i - (Ax)_i\big) = 0\;$ gives $y_i > 0 \Rightarrow (Ax)_i = b_i$. And conversely, if all terms vanish the chain collapses to equality, so both are optimal. $\blacksquare$

Worked check: the running example

Claimed optimal pair: $x^\star = (6,2)$ and $y^\star = (1,2,0)$. First, the two primal variables:

ConditionCheck
$x_1 = 6 > 0$dual constraint 1 tight?  $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 = 1+2+0 = 3 = c_1$✓
$x_2 = 2 > 0$dual constraint 2 tight?  $y_2 + 3y_3 = 2+0 = 2 = c_2$✓

ConditionCheck
$y_1 = 1 > 0$machine tight?  $x_1 = 6 = b_1$✓
$y_2 = 2 > 0$wood tight?  $x_1 + x_2 = 6+2 = 8 = b_2$✓

Worked check: the running example

All conditions hold, and both points are feasible $\Rightarrow$ both optimal — without computing either objective value.

The varnish row is the one with content: a constraint with slack must get multiplier $0$. Attempt 1 put weight on varnish, and that is exactly why it could not reach $22$.

Solving for the certificate

Given only the claimed optimum $x^\star = (6,2)$, complementary slackness hands us equations for $y^\star$:

  • varnish is slack $\Rightarrow$ $y_3 = 0$;
  • $x_2 > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $y_2 + 3y_3 = 2$, so $y_2 = 2$;
  • $x_1 > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $y_1 + y_2 + y_3 = 3$, so $y_1 = 1$.

Check $y^\star = (1,2,0) \ge 0$: it is dual feasible, so $(6,2)$ is certified. (These equations pin $y^\star$ down uniquely, so a negative entry would have meant $(6,2)$ is not optimal.)

When a price goes negative

Try complementary slackness at a vertex that is not optimal: $x = (3,5)$, value $19$. Wood and varnish are tight; the machine is slack.

  • machine slack $\Rightarrow$ $y_1 = 0$;
  • $x_1, x_2 > 0$ $\Rightarrow$ $y_2 + y_3 = 3$ and $y_2 + 3y_3 = 2$;
  • so $y_2 = \tfrac72$ and $y_3 = -\tfrac12$.

The combination $\tfrac72\,(\text{wood}) - \tfrac12\,(\text{varnish})$ gives exactly $19$ — but subtracting a $\le$ constraint is not allowed. No certificate exists at $(3,5)$.

When a price goes negative

The negative price on varnish says: loosening varnish pays.

  • Keep wood tight and move off varnish: trade one table for one chair, $(3,5) \to (4,4)$. Varnish loosens by $2$, and the objective gains $(-\tfrac12)(-2) = +1$.
  • Continue until the machine becomes tight: $(6,2)$, value $22$.
  • There the prices are $(1,2,0) \ge 0$: a valid certificate, so stop.

This illustrates how the simplex method uses prices to choose pivots. To certify a feasible point, check dual feasibility ($y \ge 0$ and $A^{\mathsf T}y \ge c$) together with complementary slackness. (Week 12's primal–dual algorithms use complementary slackness as a design principle too.)

Takeaways

  • Every LP has a dual: the LP of best bounds obtained by combining constraints. To write it for any LP, penalise each constraint with a price $y_i$: the signs and dual constraints are whatever keeps the bound valid and finite.
  • Weak duality ($c^{\mathsf T}x \le b^{\mathsf T}y$) is a one-line proof; strong duality says every finite primal optimum has a matching dual certificate.
  • Max-flow = min-cut is LP duality: the dual of the flow LP prices cuts. Two proofs, two generalisations — algorithms vs. all of LP.

Takeaways

  • Kőnig: in bipartite graphs, max matching $=$ min vertex cover — duality $+$ integrality; matching and cover certify each other.
  • Complementary slackness: nothing optimal is both used and slack. Check primal and dual feasibility too; in our example, a negative price points to an improving move.
